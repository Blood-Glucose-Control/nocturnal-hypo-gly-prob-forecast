We thank Reviewer RCHh for an exceptionally careful review. We ran **five new analyses** (reproducible in released code), all using **patient-clustered resampling**.

**Q2/W2: Significance.** Our claims are now backed by bootstrap CIs, paired tests, and a rank pipeline. (i) *"No single architecture dominates" is a cross-metric result:* on DCLP3/IOBP2 chronos2 is significantly **better** than Moirai on RMSE (Δ −0.088/−0.186) and WQL, yet significantly **worse** on DILATE-shape (Δ +58.9/+8.2) and detection AUROC (−0.068/−0.055), AUPRC (−0.032/−0.037), all paired patient-cluster (95% CI excludes 0). Spearman between the RMSE ranking and the shape/detection rankings is only **−0.05 to −0.52** vs **+0.93–0.98** for RMSE/WQL. (Detection uses the probabilistic P(BG<3.9) score, per the paper.) (ii) *Clustering:* episodes are nested in patients (interclass correlation coefficient 0.11–0.21; 167–430 patients), so we resample **patients** (design effect 2.5–6x). Under 10,000 patient-cluster bootstraps, **17 of 20** headline comparisons stay significant (the 3 exceptions are single-dataset detection ties), and the dissociation holds on every dataset. We will report effect sizes and ΔRMSE with CIs, and will add per-dataset Critical-Difference diagrams (anonymized link).

**Q1/W1: Midnight anchoring / label noise.** In Replace-BG (discrete carb entries logged), 62% of episodes have no post-midnight carb log ('normal nights'). On this subset the model AUROC ranking is preserved (Kendall τ = +0.80; the one non-identity is a virtual tie between Moirai and Toto in the full set), and per-model RMSE shifts <0.01 mmol/L. The hypo prevalence (fraction of episodes containing any BG < 3.9 mmol/L in the 8 h window) drops modestly on 'normal nights' (23.0% vs 27.4% on carb-active nights), consistent with overnight eating being a reactive response to hypo rather than random behavior, but the model comparison holds either way. Anchoring noise does not change conclusions. As more datasets become public and are paired with wearable sleep tracking data, improved anchoring labels would be a valuable addition to this benchmark, and further refinement of trying to determine sleep times is an interesting direction for future work.

**Q3/W3: Covariate fairness.** **Every model is reported glucose-only** (Tables 3–4, condition G), covariates being a per-model ablation. On the glucose-only leaderboard, **architecture spread 0.50–0.64 RMSE** >> median covariate gain **+0.07–0.10** (paired patient-cluster; chronos2 +0.09–0.18). Architecture gains do not appear to be covariate artifacts.

**Q4/W5: Subgroup / fairness.** Using enrollment age and sex, rankings are **stable** across sex (τ +1.0 on Replace-BG, IOBP2) and an age median-split (τ +1.0 on IOBP2, Tamborlane). We report gaps (e.g., DCLP3 AUROC consistently higher for female than male across all 5 evaluated models: mean 0.71 (F) vs 0.67 (M), per-model gap +0.03 to +0.08 AUROC; base rates matched at 0.12); top models stay top in every subgroup. More detailed demographic data is not available in these public datasets. We will happily add this analysis to the appendix of camera-ready.

**Q5: Do detection gains reduce missed events?** We add operating points (sens/spec/PPV/NPV + false-alarms/100). Practically, our gains are real but modest: at 90% sensitivity, 45–72 false alarms/100 nights (PPV 0.14–0.43); only trading recall (Youden) lifts PPV to 0.55–0.63. We will temper clinical claims and frame low-false-alarm long-horizon alarming as an open problem the benchmark defines.

**W4: Dataset heterogeneity.** Differences track nameable cohort/device factors: sensor (Dexcom G4/G6, iLet CGM), therapy (MDI/pump, Control-IQ, bionic pancreas), and age (Tamborlane includes children; hypo base rate 0.39 vs 0.10–0.26). These explain the CD reshuffling and deeper discussion can be added to appendix.
