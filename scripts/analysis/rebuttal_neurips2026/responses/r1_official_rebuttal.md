We thank Reviewer EfDr for engaging with the core purpose of the benchmark: that RMSE is too distal an outcome and that clinically-relevant, long-horizon, uncertainty-aware evaluation should become standard in BG forecasting.

**Q: What mistakes are the models making?** We conducted an operating-point analysis that we will include and refer to in the appendix of the paper (sensitivity/specificity/PPV/NPV + a confusion matrix at clinically meaningful thresholds). The error profile is clinically understandable, however no model is as satisfying as would be desired, for example: Moirai on DCLP3 (base rate ~12%), episode-level any-step detection:

| Operating point | TP | FP | FN | TN | Sens | Spec | PPV | NPV | FP/100 nights |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| High-sensitivity (~0.90 sens) | 417 | 2553 | 46 | 793 | 0.90 | 0.24 | 0.14 | 0.95 | 67 |
| Youden-optimal | 214 | 559 | 249 | 2787 | 0.46 | 0.83 | 0.28 | 0.92 | 15 |

In that configuration, the dominant error at deployable-sensitivity is **false alarms, not missed events** (NPV 0.92–0.95; FP ≫ FN). Tightening the threshold trades recall for a ~4.5× reduction in false alarms (67 to 15 per 100 nights) and roughly doubles PPV. Across datasets, at 90% sensitivity false alarms run 45–72/100 nights (PPV 0.14–0.43); at the Youden point PPV reaches 0.55–0.63. This is part of the **open problem the benchmark defines**, most released TSFMs (with a very limited number of exceptions) do not natively support modeling quantiles outside of the [0.1, 0.9] range. This is a critical shortcoming of the TSFM subfield, and a strong recommendation our paper makes to ML practitioners is that there are important usecases where modeling the tails of the distribution carries great importance, for our datasets the percent of CGM readings that fall below 3.9mmol\L ranges from [2.07\%, 4.58\%].
We limited the TSFMs that do support more extreme quantiles back down to [0.1. 0.9] to allow for a fair comparison of model performance, however we would be happy to add in an example of how this might improve performance in the appendix of our paper. We would recommend to practitioners working on this problem to place a heavier weight on the extremes of their WQL functions instead of balanced like ours did, but this was a scope limitation of our paper.

**W1 — Hyperparameter / compute protocol.** We agree the paper under-documents this and we will fix it. All models share an **identical evaluation harness**: context 512, horizon 96, the same preprocessing, holdout splits, and midnight-anchored episode set; only architecture-native fine-tuning differs. Each model was tuned within its own documented search space (the config files you located); we will add a **compute/HP appendix** in the camera-ready giving, per model, the search space, tuning budget, and wall-clock GPU-hours so FLOPs/clocktime comparability is explicit. We placed less focus on compute performance because this paper focused on fine-tuning instead of pre-training, we can't control the pre-training compute budgets so this is already an unfair comparison between models on that dimension, but we agree it would definitely be valuable information for people looking to work on this problem.

**W2 — "Inconclusive findings" and tradeoff discussion.** We share the goal of turning "different models win different metrics" into *actionable* guidance. We believe our rebuttal helps to make the finding concrete: chronos2 is significantly best on **RMSE/WQL**, Moirai/Toto on **shape (DILATE)**, and Moirai/PatchTST on **event detection (AUROC/AUPRC)**. We performed a paired patient-clustered double dissociation (Spearman between the RMSE ranking and the shape/detection rankings is only −0.05 to −0.52, vs +0.93–0.98 for RMSE↔WQL). From our paper a practitioner should takeaway the following: **select the model by the clinical objective, not by RMSE.** The mechanism (continuous-output models place probability mass in the hypoglycemic tail, which point-optimized models smooth away) motivates a concrete future direction: training with expanded/weighted extreme quantiles rather than the default 0.1–0.9 grid.
